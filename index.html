The essentials, at a glance

No slide-by-slide walkthrough here — just the definitions, laws, and numbers worth knowing cold.

Solubility

The maximum concentration of a solute that can be dissolved in a given solvent, at a given temperature, to form a solution — the solute's ability to form a solution with the solvent.

Rate of dissolution

The speed at which the solute goes into solution. Formula: Rate = ΔC / ΔT (mg/mL/min). Higher solubility raises dissolution rate — but a faster dissolution rate does not always mean higher solubility.

UnsaturatedSolvent can still dissolve more solute.
SaturatedMaximum solute dissolved; excess solid in equilibrium.
SupersaturatedMore solute than normal — made by heating, dissolving, then cooling.

5 factors affecting solubility of a solid in a liquid

  • Temperature — endothermic ↑solubility, exothermic ↓solubility, NaCl is unaffected.
  • Molecular structure — polar, non-polar, and semipolar (ethanol) liquids.
  • Nature of solute — polymorphism, hydrates, and solvates.
  • pH — weak acids dissolve better at high pH, weak bases at low pH.
  • Other substances — salting-in raises solubility, salting-out (common ion) lowers it.

Henry's Law

S(gas) ∝ P

At constant temperature, gas solubility by weight is proportional to pressure. CO₂ is about twice as soluble at 0°C as at 20°C.

NaCl reference point

1 g / 2.8 mL

1 g of NaCl dissolves in 2.8 mL of water — placing it in the "freely soluble" category (1–10 parts solvent).

High-yield for the exam

If you only have ten minutes before the test, this is what to reread. Teal = core concept, gold = a number worth memorizing, brick = a common trap.

Must-know concept Number to memorize Common exam trap
Definition

Solubility = the maximum concentration of solute that a given solvent can hold at a given temperature — i.e. the solute's ability to form a solution with the solvent.

Trap

Unsaturated vs. saturated vs. supersaturated — supersaturated is NOT just "very saturated." It is made by heating, dissolving extra solute, then cooling undisturbed. It holds more solute than the temperature would normally allow.

List to memorize in order

5 factors affecting solubility of a solid in a liquid: 1) Temperature 2) Molecular structure 3) Nature of solute 4) pH 5) Other substances.

Trap

Endothermic ≠ Exothermic direction. Endothermic dissolution (+heat of solution) → solubility ↑ with temperature. Exothermic (−heat of solution) → solubility ↓ with temperature. NaCl is the classic exception — its solubility barely changes with temperature.

Concept

Like dissolves like. Polar liquids dissolve electrovalent/polar compounds; non-polar liquids are needed for non-polar solutes. Ethanol is semipolar: 5 non-polar C–H bonds + 1 non-polar C–C bond, but a polar C–O and O–H bond — so it bridges both worlds.

Named example

Polymorphism: same chemical structure, different physical form (melting point, solubility, activity). Classic exam example: Chloramphenicol — one polymorph is active, another inactive.

Methods list

Techniques to detect polymorphism/crystal form: hot stage microscopy, thermal analysis, IR spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction.

Trap

Anhydrous vs. hydrate: the anhydrous form is more soluble than the hydrate form of the same compound (the hydrate's lattice already "used up" some of the attraction to water). Ampicillin trihydrate = 3 water molecules per unit.

Rule + example

pH: weak acids (e.g. sulfonamides) are more soluble as pH rises (add base → soluble salt); weak bases (e.g. alkaloids) are more soluble as pH falls (add acid → soluble salt). Phenobarbital free acid precipitates below pH 5.5 and dissolves better above it.

Number

Phenobarbital solubility threshold: pH 5.5. Below it, the free acid precipitates.

Trap — opposite effects

Salting-in (electrolyte added to a non-electrolyte solution) → solubility ↑. Salting-out (a soluble salt shares a common ion with a slightly soluble salt) → that slightly soluble salt precipitates, solubility ↓. Classic pair: adding NaCl to saturated AgCl precipitates AgCl via the common Cl⁻ ion.

Number

NaCl reference point: 1 g dissolves in 2.8 mL water → falls in the "freely soluble" band (1–10 parts solvent per part solute).

Table to memorize

Solubility bands (parts solvent per part solute): very soluble <1, freely soluble 1–10, soluble 10–30, sparingly soluble 30–100, slightly soluble 100–1000, very slightly soluble 1000–10,000, practically insoluble >10,000.

Trap — opposite of solids

Henry's Law (gas in liquid): solubility of a gas ∝ pressure at constant temperature — but unlike most solids, raising temperature decreases gas solubility. CO₂ is about twice as soluble at 0 °C as at 20 °C.

Formula

Rate of dissolution = ΔC / ΔT (mg/mL/min) — the speed solute enters solution, not the same thing as solubility.

Trap — one-way relationship

Solubility ↑ always → dissolution rate ↑. But dissolution rate ↑ (via particle size, agitation, heat) does not always mean solubility ↑ — you can speed up how fast the same maximum amount dissolves.

List to memorize

3 factors that speed up dissolution rate: smaller particle size (more surface area), agitation (fresh solvent reaches the surface), heating (more frequent solvent–solute collisions).

Glossary of defined terms

Solubility
Maximum concentration of a solute a solvent can hold at a given temperature; the solute's ability to form a solution with the solvent.
Saturated solubility
The maximum amount of solute dissolved when excess solid is shaken with a solvent for a period of time.
Unsaturated solution
A solution that can still dissolve more solute at that temperature.
Supersaturated solution
Contains more dissolved solute than normal, made by heating, dissolving excess solute, then cooling undisturbed.
Endothermic reaction
Dissolution that has a positive heat of solution; solubility increases as temperature rises.
Exothermic reaction
Dissolution that has a negative heat of solution; solubility decreases as temperature rises.
Polar liquids
Miscible with other polar liquids, dissolve electrovalent compounds, and form H-bonds with solute (e.g. water, low-MWt alcohols).
Non-polar liquids
Required to dissolve non-polar solutes ("like dissolves like").
Semipolar liquids
Show properties of both polar and non-polar solvents, e.g. ethanol (non-polar C–H/C–C bonds plus polar C–O/O–H bonds).
Polymorphism
A compound existing in more than one crystal form with the same chemical structure but different melting point, solubility, absorption, or activity (e.g. chloramphenicol).
Hydrates
Compounds that contain water within their crystal structure (mono-, di-, trihydrate), e.g. ampicillin trihydrate.
Solvates
Compounds that contain a solvent other than water within their crystal structure.
Salting-in
Adding an electrolyte to a non-electrolyte solution, causing an increase in solubility.
Salting-out
Adding a soluble salt that shares a common ion with a slightly soluble salt, causing the slightly soluble salt to precipitate.
Henry's Law
The solubility of a gas by weight in a liquid is proportional to pressure at a constant temperature.
Rate of dissolution
The speed at which solute enters solution: Rate = ΔC/ΔT (mg/mL/min).
Very soluble
<1 part solvent needed per part solute.
Freely soluble
1–10 parts solvent needed per part solute (e.g. NaCl).
Soluble
10–30 parts solvent needed per part solute.
Sparingly soluble
30–100 parts solvent needed per part solute.
Slightly soluble
100–1000 parts solvent needed per part solute.
Very slightly soluble
1000–10,000 parts solvent needed per part solute.
Practically insoluble
>10,000 parts solvent needed per part solute.
