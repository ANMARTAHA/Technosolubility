01

Core Idea

Solubility is the maximum concentration to which a solution can be prepared from a given solute and solvent. It is governed by temperature, molecular structure, nature of the solute, pH, and other substances (solids in liquids) and by pressure (gases in liquids, Henry's law), while the rate of dissolution describes how fast the solute goes into solution.
02

Learning Points

  • Solubility sets the maximum concentration obtainable for a given agent and solvent; a saturated solution holds the maximum dissolved amount.
  • Adding more solute to a saturated solution and raising the temperature dissolves more solute, giving a supersaturated solution.
  • Five factors affect the solubility of a solid in a liquid: temperature, molecular structure, nature of solute, pH, and other substances.
  • Temperature effect depends on the heat of solution: endothermic (positive) ↑ solubility, exothermic (negative) ↓ solubility, and some compounds (e.g. NaCl) are unaffected.
  • Polar solvents dissolve polar/electrovalent solutes; non-polar solvents are required for non-polar solutes; semipolar solvents (ethanol) have both properties.
  • Polymorphism, hydrates, and solvates change the physical properties — including solubility — of the same compound.
  • Weak acids dissolve better as pH rises; weak bases dissolve better as pH falls — through formation of water-soluble salts.
  • Salting-in raises solubility; salting-out (common-ion effect) lowers it.
  • Solubility can be described with relative terms based on parts of solvent needed per one part of solute.
  • Henry's law: gas solubility is proportional to pressure at constant temperature, and gas solubility falls as temperature rises.
  • Rate of dissolution = ΔC/ΔT; it is raised by smaller particle size, agitation, and heating.
03

Key Definitions

Solubility (definition 1)

The solubility of an agent in a particular solvent indicates the maximum concentration to which a solution may be prepared with that agent and that solvent.

Solubility (definition 2)

The ability of a solute substance to form a solution with another substance (the solvent).

Saturated solubility

When an excess of solid (solute) is shaken with a liquid (solvent) for a time, a maximum amount of it will be dissolved.

Supersaturated solution

When an excess amount of solute is added to a saturated solution and the temperature is increased, more solute will be dissolved.

Polymorphism

A phenomenon in which a compound exists in more than one form that has the same chemical structure but different physical properties (melting point, solubility, absorption, therapeutic activity).

Hydrates

Compounds that contain water in their crystals.

Solvates

Compounds that contain a solvent other than water in their crystals.

Salting-in

Addition of an electrolyte to a non-electrolyte solution causes an increase in solubility.

Salting-out

Addition of a soluble salt to a solution of a slightly soluble salt that has a common ion causes precipitation of the slightly soluble salt (decreased solubility).

Henry's law

The solubility of a gas by weight in a liquid is proportional to the pressure at constant temperature.

Rate of dissolution

The speed at which the solute goes into solution. Rate of dissolution = ΔC/ΔT = mg/ml/min.

05

Important Concepts

1. Temperature

Endothermic: increase in temperature leads to increased solubility (positive heat of solution).

Exothermic: increase in temperature results in decreased solubility (negative heat of solution).

Neither: solubility at low temperature equals solubility at high temperature (e.g. NaCl).

2a. Polar liquids

Miscible with other polar liquids, slightly miscible with non-polar liquids, and dissolve electrovalent compounds.

Solutes are capable of H-bond formation with the solvent (e.g. water and alcohol of low MWt).

As the MWt of the alcohol increases, polarity decreases and solubility in water decreases.

Water dissolves salts and other electrolytes readily, so polar liquids are poor solvents for non-polar substances.

2b. Non-polar & semipolar liquids

Non-polar liquids are required for non-polar solutes.

Semipolar liquids, such as ethyl alcohol, possess some of the properties of both polar and non-polar solvents.

Ethanol: 5 non-polar C–H bonds, 1 C–C bond (non-polar), C–O bond and H–O bond (polar). It forms a hydrogen bond with water.

3a. Polymorphism

Same chemical structure but different physical properties: melting point, solubility, absorption, and therapeutic activity.

Some polymorphs are inactive, so the pharmacist should be aware of this phenomenon.

The lecture poses "Why does a solute suffer from polymorphism?" as a discussion question; no answer is given in the slides.

3b. Hydrates & solvates

Hydrates may be monohydrate, dihydrate, or trihydrate. The anhydrous form of a compound is more soluble than the hydrate form of the same compound.

Solvates: the solvate of the same compound is more soluble than the non-solvate form in water.

Both "Why?" questions are left open in the slides.

4. pH

Organic substances are either weak acids or weak bases; their aqueous solubility depends on the pH of the solvent.

Weak organic acids (e.g. sulfonamides): solubility increases as pH increases (addition of base), due to formation of water-soluble salts.

Weak organic bases (alkaloids): solubility increases as pH decreases (addition of acid), due to formation of water-soluble salts.

5. Other substances

Salting-in: an electrolyte added to a non-electrolyte solution increases solubility.

Salting-out: a soluble salt (NaCl, LiBr, KI, NH4NO3, BaCl2, MgI2, Na2SO4, Na3PO4, NaNO2) added to a solution of a slightly soluble salt (CaSO4, BaSO4, BiPO4) with a common ion causes its precipitation.

Gas in liquid — Henry's law

Gas solubility by weight is proportional to pressure at constant temperature (low pressure → less gas dissolved; high pressure → more).

Increasing the temperature decreases gas solubility; CO2 is twice as soluble at 0 °C as at 20 °C.

Rate of dissolution

Particle size: reduction in particle size increases surface area (S.A.) and rate of dissolution.

Agitation: removes the more concentrated solution from the solute surface and brings in less concentrated solvent, increasing the rate.

Heating: increases solubility by raising the frequency with which solvent molecules collide with the surface of the dissolving material.

07

Processes / Stages

From unsaturated to supersaturated

  1. An excess of solid (solute) is shaken with liquid (solvent) for a time.
  2. A maximum amount dissolves → saturated solution.
  3. Excess solute is added to the saturated solution and the temperature is increased.
  4. More solute dissolves → supersaturated solution. Usually done by heating, dissolving more solute, and cooling.

Phenobarbital and pH

  1. pH is raised above 5.5 by adding a strong base → solubility increases.
  2. pH is lowered by adding a strong acid → phenobarbital (free acid) precipitates.

Salting-out with AgCl

  1. NaCl is added to a saturated solution of silver chloride (AgCl).
  2. The common ion (Cl−) is present.
  3. Some AgCl precipitates from the solution.

Effect of agitation on dissolution

  1. Agitation removes the more concentrated solution from the surface of the solute.
  2. A less concentrated solvent is brought in.
  3. Rate of dissolution increases.

09

Examples

  • NaCl — neither exothermic nor endothermic (same solubility at low and high temperature); also "1 g of NaCl dissolves in 2.8 ml of water".
  • Water and alcohol of low MWt — H-bond formation with polar solvent; water dissolves salts and electrolytes readily.
  • Ethyl alcohol (ethanol) — semipolar: 5 non-polar C–H bonds, 1 C–C bond; polar C–O and H–O bonds.
  • Chloramphenicol — exists in more than one polymorph; one active and the other inactive.
  • Ampicillin trihydrate — contains three molecules of water.
  • Sulfonamides (weak organic acids) and alkaloids (weak organic bases).
  • Phenobarbital — solubility increases above pH 5.5; free acid precipitates when pH is lowered.
  • Salting-out salts: NaCl, LiBr, KI, NH4NO3, BaCl2, MgI2, Na2SO4, Na3PO4, NaNO2; slightly soluble salts: CaSO4, BaSO4, BiPO4.
  • AgCl + NaCl — precipitation due to common ion Cl−.
  • CO2 — twice as soluble at 0 °C as at 20 °C.
10

Clinical / Practical Importance

  • Polymorphs differ in melting point, solubility, absorption, and therapeutic activity; some are inactive, so the pharmacist should be aware (e.g. chloramphenicol).
  • Crystal properties are determined by hot stage microscopy, thermal analysis, infrared spectroscopy, and X-ray diffraction.
  • Adjusting pH changes solubility through salt formation; lowering pH of a phenobarbital solution precipitates the free acid.
  • Relative terms of solubility give a standard way to describe solubility (parts of solvent per one part of solute).
  • Particle size reduction, agitation, and heating are the practical ways to increase the rate of dissolution.
